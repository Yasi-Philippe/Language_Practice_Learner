# Reading list

Books originally written in Italian, by Italian authors, grouped by level. Inside each level they're in the recommended order — start at the top.
The app shows this list on the **Books** screen, where you can mark each one as *reading* or *read*.

Rules for editing:
- One `## <level> · <short description>` heading per level, then a table `Title | Author | Year | Why this level | Curiosity`.
- A book's reading status is stored under its **title** — don't rename a title once you've marked the book.

## A1 · First steps: comics and rhymes

| Title | Author | Year | Why this level | Curiosity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Topolino | Various Italian authors | 1949 | The weekly Italian Mickey Mouse comic. Pictures carry the meaning; the dialogue is short, everyday and mostly in the present. One story a week is plenty. | Italy produces more Disney comics than anywhere else. Italian authors even gave Donald Duck a superhero alter ego, **Paperinik**, in 1969. |
| Lupo Alberto | Silver | 1974 | Comic strips of a few panels: one joke, a handful of words, lots of everyday expressions. | Alberto is a blue wolf in love with Marta the hen, forever chased off her farm by the sheepdog Mosè. |
| Filastrocche in cielo e in terra | Gianni Rodari | 1960 | Short nursery rhymes. Rhythm and rhyme make the words stick — read them out loud. | Rodari is the only Italian writer to have won the Hans Christian Andersen Award (1970), the "Nobel" of children's literature. |

## A2 · Short stories you can finish

| Title | Author | Year | Why this level | Curiosity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Il mistero dell'occhio di smeraldo | Elisabetta Dami (as Geronimo Stilton) | 2000 | The first Geronimo Stilton adventure: short chapters, lots of dialogue, key words printed in big colored letters. | In Spain he's *Gerónimo Stilton* — if you read him as a child, you already know the story, which makes it a perfect first Italian book. |
| Favole al telefono | Gianni Rodari | 1962 | Seventy stories of one or two pages: lots of **c'era una volta**, imperfetto and passato prossimo. | Every evening a travelling salesman from Varese tells his daughter a story by phone — short ones, because he pays for the calls himself. |
| Il libro degli errori | Gianni Rodari | 1964 | Rhymes and stories built on spelling and grammar mistakes — a funny companion to your grammar path. | It opens with: *"Gli errori sono necessari, utili come il pane e spesso anche belli: per esempio, la torre di Pisa."* |
| Le avventure di Cipollino | Gianni Rodari | 1951 | A brave little onion against Prince Lemon: a fable with clear sentences and a whole garden of vocabulary. | First published as *Il romanzo di Cipollino*, it became hugely popular in the Soviet Union — as a cartoon and even a ballet. |

## B1 · Your first real novels

| Title | Author | Year | Why this level | Curiosity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Marcovaldo | Italo Calvino | 1963 | Twenty short, funny, poetic stories about a poor worker looking for nature in the grey city. Each one stands alone. | The stories follow the seasons — spring, summer, autumn, winter — five times round. |
| Seta | Alessandro Baricco | 1996 | About a hundred pages, short chapters, short sentences that come back like a refrain. Your first whole novel in Italian. | A French silkworm trader crosses the world to Japan in the 1860s. It became the film *Silk* (2007). |
| Le avventure di Pinocchio | Carlo Collodi | 1883 | The original, not the cartoon: short chapters, lots of dialogue, a few old Tuscan words. | It opens by teasing the reader: *"C'era una volta… — Un re! — diranno subito i miei piccoli lettori. No, ragazzi, avete sbagliato. C'era una volta un pezzo di legno."* |
| Io non ho paura | Niccolò Ammaniti | 2001 | Told by nine-year-old Michele, in simple language, one summer in the South. Impossible to put down. | Gabriele Salvatores turned it into a film in 2003 — watch it after reading. |
| Nel mare ci sono i coccodrilli | Fabio Geda | 2010 | A true story told as a conversation, in plain spoken Italian. | It's the journey of Enaiatollah Akbari, who left Afghanistan as a child and reached Italy alone. |
| Va' dove ti porta il cuore | Susanna Tamaro | 1994 | A grandmother's letters to her granddaughter: first person, simple and emotional. | The title is the imperative you drilled: **Va'** = **vai**. One of the best-selling Italian novels ever. |

## B2 · Modern classics

| Title | Author | Year | Why this level | Curiosity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| L'amica geniale | Elena Ferrante | 2011 | Two girls growing up in 1950s Naples: long, gripping, contemporary Italian. | Nobody officially knows who Elena Ferrante is — the name is a pen name. |
| Le otto montagne | Paolo Cognetti | 2016 | Friendship and mountains, in clear and quiet prose. | It won the Premio Strega in 2017; the film won the Jury Prize at Cannes in 2022. |
| Sostiene Pereira | Antonio Tabucchi | 1994 | Lisbon, 1938: a journalist slowly finds his courage. The refrain *"sostiene Pereira"* carries you along. | Tabucchi loved Portugal so much that he wrote one of his novels, *Requiem*, in Portuguese. |
| Lessico famigliare | Natalia Ginzburg | 1963 | A family in Turin told through its catchphrases — a book literally about words. | It won the Premio Strega in 1963. |
| Il barone rampante | Italo Calvino | 1957 | Witty, clear and full of adventure. | At twelve, Cosimo climbs a tree after refusing a plate of snails — and never comes down again. |
| Il deserto dei Tartari | Dino Buzzati | 1940 | Plain sentences with a deep effect. | A young officer waits his whole life at a frontier fort for an enemy that never comes. |

## C1 · The great 20th century

| Title | Author | Year | Why this level | Curiosity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Se questo è un uomo | Primo Levi | 1947 | Auschwitz, told with a scientist's precision: clear language, heavy content. | Levi was a chemist; in his book *Il sistema periodico* every chapter is named after an element. |
| Il fu Mattia Pascal | Luigi Pirandello | 1904 | Ironic and sharp, in slightly old-fashioned Italian. | A man reads about his own death in the paper and starts a new life. Pirandello won the Nobel Prize in 1934. |
| Le città invisibili | Italo Calvino | 1972 | Short, poetic chapters with a rich vocabulary. | Marco Polo describes 55 imaginary cities to Kublai Khan. |
| La forma dell'acqua | Andrea Camilleri | 1994 | The first Inspector Montalbano novel: Italian seasoned with Sicilian. | Montalbano is named after the Spanish writer Manuel Vázquez Montalbán. |
| Il nome della rosa | Umberto Eco | 1980 | Murders in a medieval abbey, with Latin quotations and philosophy. | Eco was a professor of semiotics; this was his first novel, published when he was 48. |
| La coscienza di Zeno | Italo Svevo | 1923 | A diary written for a psychoanalyst — funny and painfully honest. | Zeno keeps smoking his *"ultima sigaretta"*, his last cigarette, over and over again. |

## C2 · The summit

| Title | Author | Year | Why this level | Curiosity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Il Gattopardo | Giuseppe Tomasi di Lampedusa | 1958 | Long sentences, aristocratic Sicily, the unification of Italy. | Publishers rejected it during the author's lifetime; it came out after his death and won the Premio Strega in 1959. *"Se vogliamo che tutto rimanga come è, bisogna che tutto cambi."* |
| I promessi sposi | Alessandro Manzoni | 1840 | The novel that shaped modern Italian, in 19th-century language. | Manzoni moved to Florence to "rinse his clothes in the Arno" — and rewrote the whole novel in Florentine Italian. |
| La Divina Commedia | Dante Alighieri | c. 1320 | Seven hundred years old and still (mostly) readable — with a good commentary. | Italians quote it every day: *"Lasciate ogne speranza, voi ch'intrate."* |
| I Malavoglia | Giovanni Verga | 1881 | A family of fishermen in Sicily; Italian bent to the rhythm of Sicilian speech. | Verga tried to vanish as an author and let the village itself tell the story. |
| Quer pasticciaccio brutto de via Merulana | Carlo Emilio Gadda | 1957 | A crime in Rome told in a whirlwind of dialects, Latin and invented words — the final boss. | The title itself is in Roman dialect: "that awful mess on Via Merulana". |
