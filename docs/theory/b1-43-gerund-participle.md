# One word, a whole clause

> **Sbagliando s'impara** — you learn by making mistakes. Gerunds and participles pack an entire clause into one word, in both languages.

## La regola

| form | means | example |
|---|---|---|
| gerund **-ando / -endo** | while, by, since | **camminando**, **studiando** |
| past gerund **avendo / essendo** + participle | having done | **avendo finito**, **essendo arrivati** |
| **pur** + gerund | even though | **pur essendo stanco** |
| past participle + noun | once … done | **finito il lavoro**, **scritta la lettera** |

## Come in spagnolo

- The gerund for how, while, by: **imparo leggendo** = *aprendo leyendo*.
- The participle clause is identical: **finito il lavoro, siamo usciti** = *terminado el trabajo, salimos*.
- **pur essendo** = *aun siendo*.

## Diverso dallo spagnolo

- One ending for **-ere** and **-ire**: **-endo** — **dormendo** (ES *durmiendo*), **leggendo** (ES *leyendo*).
- The participle clause agrees with its noun: **scritta la lettera, fatti i compiti, preparate le valigie**.
- The gerund shares its subject with the main verb: **leggendo, ho imparato** — I read, I learned.

## Occhio!

- Irregular gerunds come from the old Latin stems: **facendo, dicendo, bevendo** (*facere, dicere, bibere*).
- **pur** + gerund for "although": **pur sapendo tutto, non ha detto niente**.

## Curiosità

That participle clause — **finito il lavoro** — is a two-thousand-year-old Latin construction, the *ablative absolute* (*urbe condita*, "the city having been founded"). Italian and Spanish both inherited it intact, which is why **detto fatto** and *dicho y hecho* mean exactly the same thing: said, and done.

## Il trucco

If Spanish can say it with a gerund or a lone participle, Italian usually can too: *terminado el trabajo* → **finito il lavoro**; *caminando* → **camminando**.
