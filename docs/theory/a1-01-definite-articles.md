# Seven ways to say "the"

> Spanish gets by with *el, la, los, las*. Italian needs seven words — and it's the **next word** that decides which one.

## La regola

| The next word starts with… | singular (m.) | plural (m.) |
|---|---|---|
| a normal consonant | **il** libro | **i** libri |
| s + consonant, z, gn, ps, y | **lo** zaino | **gli** zaini |
| a vowel | **l'**amico | **gli** amici |

Feminine is the easy half: **la** casa → **le** case, and **l'**amica → **le** amiche.

## Come in spagnolo

- The article agrees in gender and number with its noun, exactly like *el / la / los / las*.
- Both languages hate two vowels bumping into each other: Spanish says *el agua*, Italian says **l'acqua**. Same problem, different patch.
- Like Spanish, Italian uses the article for body parts and big ideas: *me duele la cabeza* → **mi fa male la testa**, *el amor* → **l'amore**.

## Diverso dallo spagnolo

- The article depends on how the next word **sounds**, not only on gender: **il** treno but **lo** zaino — both masculine.
- Gender often flips between the two languages: *la mochila* → **lo zaino**, *la leche* → **il latte**, *la nariz* → **il naso**, *la sangre* → **il sangue**, *el fin* → **la fine**.
- Plural **le** never takes an apostrophe: **le arance**, never *l'arance*.

## Occhio!

- The article looks only at the very next word. Put an adjective in between and it changes: **lo** studente but **il** bravo studente; **gli** amici but **i** vecchi amici.
- **gli dei** (the gods) is the famous exception: it comes from the old form *gl'iddei*, and the article stayed.

## Curiosità

**lo** used to be everywhere. Dante opens the second canto of the *Inferno* with "**Lo** giorno se n'andava" — today that's **il** giorno. Modern Italian kept **lo** only where **il** would trip over a cluster: try saying *il studente*. Spanish solved the same clash the other way round: it put an *e* in front of the word — *estudiante, escuela, espejo* — while Italian left the word alone and changed the article: **lo studente, la scuola, lo specchio**.

## Il trucco

A Spanish word starting with **es-** + consonant usually has an Italian twin starting with **s-** + consonant — and if it's masculine, it takes **lo / gli**: *estudiante* → **lo studente**, *espejo* → **lo specchio**, *estómago* → **lo stomaco**, *espacio* → **lo spazio**.
