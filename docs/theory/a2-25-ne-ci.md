# Ne and ci: the words Spanish dropped

> Two tiny words with no Spanish equivalent. **ne** counts, **ci** points — and once you notice them, you'll hear them in every Italian sentence.

## La regola

- **ne** = "of it / of them", with quantities: **Quante mele? Ne voglio tre.** Also "about it": **ne parliamo**.
- **ci** = "there": **Vai a Roma? Sì, ci vado.** Also "about / in it" after verbs with **a** or **in**: **ci penso** (*pienso en ello*), **ci credo** (*lo creo*).
- **ci vuole** + singular, **ci vogliono** + plural = "it takes": **ci vuole un'ora**, **ci vogliono due ore**.
- **metterci** = "to take" when a person takes time: **ci metto venti minuti**.

## Come in spagnolo

- **ci vuole** ≈ *hace falta*: **per la pizza ci vuole la farina** = *para la pizza hace falta harina*.
- **metterci** ≈ *tardar*: **quanto ci metti?** = *¿cuánto tardas?*

## Diverso dallo spagnolo

- Spanish just leaves the "of them" out: *¿Cuántos hermanos tienes? Tengo dos.* Italian can't: **Ne ho due.**
- Same with age: *tengo treinta* → **ne ho trenta**.
- **ci** repeats a place Spanish would leave unsaid: *¿Vas a Roma? Sí, voy mañana* → **Sì, ci vado domani**.

## Occhio!

- With **ne** in the passato prossimo, the participle agrees with what you counted: (le uova) **ne ho comprate sei**.
- **c'è** is **ci** + **è**: the very same **ci**.

## Curiosità

Spanish once had its own **ne**: *ende*, from Latin *inde*, "from there" — the same root as Italian **ne** and French *en*. It survives in a fossil you use without thinking: *por ende*, "therefore", literally "for that". Catalan never lost it: *en vull dos* = **ne voglio due**.

## Il trucco

If Spanish answers with a bare number (*quiero tres*), Italian puts **ne** in front. If Spanish could add *allí*, Italian adds **ci**.
