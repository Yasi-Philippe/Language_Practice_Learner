# Asking, denying, and "there is"

> Questions look like statements, double negatives are fine, and **c'è** is a "there is" with the "there" still showing.

## La regola

- Question words: **chi** (*quién*), **che / cosa / che cosa** (*qué*), **dove** (*dónde*), **quando** (*cuándo*), **come** (*cómo*), **perché** (*por qué*), **quanto, quanta, quanti, quante** (*cuánto*…), **quale, quali** (*cuál, qué*).
- Negation: **non** + verb. **non… mai** (never), **non… niente** (nothing), **non… nessuno** (nobody).
- There is / there are: **c'è** + singular, **ci sono** + plural.

## Come in spagnolo

- Double negation works the same: **non ho visto nessuno** = *no he visto a nadie*. And if the negative word comes first, **non** disappears in both: **nessuno è venuto** = *nadie ha venido*.
- **quanto** agrees like *cuánto*: **quante ore?** = *¿cuántas horas?*
- **perché** is *por qué* and *porque* in one word: **Perché? Perché sì!** = *¿Por qué? ¡Porque sí!*

## Diverso dallo spagnolo

- No upside-down question mark: **Parli italiano?** — only your voice tells it's a question.
- *Hay* never changes; Italian agrees: **c'è un problema**, **ci sono due problemi**.
- **come** is both *cómo* and *como*: **come stai?**, **bianco come la neve**.

## Occhio!

- **chi** is *quién* — not *qué*: **Chi è?** = *¿Quién es?*
- **c'è molta gente**: **gente** is singular in Italian too, so **c'è**, never *ci sono*.

## Curiosità

Spanish *hay* hides a "there" inside: it's *ha* plus an old word *y*, "there" (from Latin *ibi*). Italian **c'è** is built the same way — **ci** ("there") + **è** — Italian just left the pieces visible. And the opening *¿* you write every day is a Spanish invention: the Real Academia recommended it in 1754, and almost no other language followed.

## Il trucco

To turn a statement into a question, raise your voice at the end. **Hai fame.** → **Hai fame?**
