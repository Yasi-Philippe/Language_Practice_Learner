# What if? Unreal conditions

> **Se fossi ricco, comprerei una barca.** Italian builds its "what ifs" exactly like Spanish — around one golden rule that trips up even Italians.

## La regola

| | condition (se…) | result |
|---|---|---|
| unreal present | **se** + imperfect subjunctive: **se avessi tempo** | present conditional: **verrei** |
| unreal past | **se** + pluperfect subjunctive: **se avessi saputo** | past conditional: **sarei venuto** |
| mixed | **se** + pluperfect subjunctive: **se avessi studiato** | present conditional: **ora saprei** |

## Come in spagnolo

- The same mapping: *si tuviera… iría* = **se avessi… andrei**; *si hubiera sabido… habría venido* = **se avessi saputo… sarei venuto**.
- **se fossi in te** = *yo que tú* — literally "if I were in you".

## Diverso dallo spagnolo

- The result clause is always a conditional: **sarei venuto**, never *fossi venuto* (Spanish allows *hubiera venido* there).
- Only the **-sse** forms exist (**avessi, fossi**); there's no *-ra* alternative.

## Occhio!

- The cardinal sin is a conditional after **se**: *se sarei ricco* ✗ → **se fossi ricco**. Italian teachers circle it in red.
- **c'è** becomes **se ci fosse**: **se non ci fosse traffico**.

## Curiosità

Spanish has its own version of the cardinal sin. In parts of northern Spain — the Basque Country, Navarre, La Rioja — you'll hear *si tendría dinero, iría*: exactly the mistake Italian teachers hunt down as *se avrei*. Two languages, one temptation: the conditional keeps trying to sneak into the "if".

## Il trucco

Whatever Spanish puts after *si* (*tuviera, hubiera sabido*), Italian puts after **se** (**avessi, avessi saputo**). Whatever Spanish puts in the result (*iría, habría venido*), so does Italian (**andrei, sarei venuto**).
