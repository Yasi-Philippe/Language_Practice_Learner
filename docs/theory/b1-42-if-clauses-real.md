# "Se": real conditions

> If it rains, we stay home. Real conditions work in Italian much as in Spanish — with one freedom Spanish doesn't have.

## La regola

| condition | result | example |
|---|---|---|
| **se** + present | present | **se piove, resto a casa** |
| **se** + present | future | **se piove, resterò a casa** |
| **se** + present | imperative | **se lo vedi, salutalo** |
| **se** + future | future | **se studierai, passerai** |

## Come in spagnolo

- **se** = *si*, with the same combinations: **se hai tempo, chiamami** = *si tienes tiempo, llámame*.
- Imperative in the result, as in Spanish: **se vedi Marco, digli…** = *si ves a Marco, dile…*

## Diverso dallo spagnolo

- Italian may put the future after **se** when the condition lies in the future: **se verrai, ti divertirai**. Spanish never does: *si vendrás* ✗.
- The two little words swap: Spanish *si* ("if") is Italian **se**, and Spanish *se* is Italian **si**.

## Occhio!

- Mixing present and future is fine (**se studi, passerai**). What's never fine is a conditional after **se**.
- **fare tardi** = *llegar tarde*: **se faccio tardi, non aspettatemi**.

## Curiosità

Italians answer uncertainty with a proverb built on this very structure: **Se son rose, fioriranno** — "if they're roses, they'll bloom", meaning *time will tell*. It's what you say about a new romance; Spain would say *el tiempo lo dirá*.

## Il trucco

Spanish *si* + present → Italian **se** + present. Talking about the future? You may switch both halves to the future.
