# One apostrophe, one gender

> Four little words — **un, uno, una, un'** — and a tiny apostrophe that tells you the gender before you even read the noun.

## La regola

| The next word starts with… | masculine | feminine |
|---|---|---|
| a consonant | **un** libro | **una** casa |
| s + consonant, z, gn, ps, y | **uno** zaino | **una** zucchina |
| a vowel | **un** amico | **un'**amica |

## Come in spagnolo

- *un / una* → **un / una**: same words, same agreement.
- Spanish also bends its rule before a stressed *a-*: *un águila, un hacha*. Italian simply uses the apostrophe: **un'aquila**.

## Diverso dallo spagnolo

- **uno** is an article here, before s + consonant, z, gn, ps, y: **uno studente**. In Spanish *uno* is only the number or a pronoun.
- There is no plural *unos / unas*. Italian says **dei / degli / delle**, **alcuni**, or nothing at all: *tengo unos libros* → **ho dei libri**, **ho alcuni libri**.

## Occhio!

- The apostrophe only goes with the **feminine**: **un amico** (m.) but **un'amica** (f.). Write *un'amico* and every Italian teacher in the country winces.
- That makes the apostrophe a free clue: **un'insegnante** is a female teacher, **un insegnante** a male one.

## Curiosità

The same logic is behind the most famous spelling mistake in Italy: **qual è** has no apostrophe, because **qual** can stand on its own — like **un** — it isn't a clipped **quale**. Still, *qual'è* turns up everywhere, and the Accademia della Crusca, the guardian of the language, keeps having to explain it.

## Il trucco

Read the apostrophe as an **a** that fell off: **un'amica** = *una amica*. No **a** to lose, no apostrophe: **un amico**.
