# Two verbs doing the work of four

> Spanish splits *being* into *ser* and *estar*, and *having* into *tener* and *haber*. Italian merges each pair into one: **essere** and **avere**.

## La regola

| | essere | avere |
|---|---|---|
| io | **sono** | **ho** |
| tu | **sei** | **hai** |
| lui / lei / Lei | **è** | **ha** |
| noi | **siamo** | **abbiamo** |
| voi | **siete** | **avete** |
| loro | **sono** | **hanno** |

## Come in spagnolo

- **avere** with feelings works just like *tener*: **ho fame, sete, sonno, freddo, caldo, paura, ragione** = *tengo hambre, sed, sueño, frío, calor, miedo, razón*.
- Age too: **ho trent'anni** = *tengo treinta años*.
- **sono** is both "I am" and "they are" — Spanish has the same double job in *era* (*yo era / él era*).

## Diverso dallo spagnolo

- **essere** = *ser* + *estar*: **sono stanco** (*estoy*), **sono italiano** (*soy*), **sono a casa** (*estoy*).
- **stare** exists, but for how you feel (**come stai?**), for staying put, and in **sto mangiando** — not for where things are.
- "There is" is built with **essere**: **c'è, ci sono** — Spanish uses *haber* (*hay*).

## Occhio!

- **è** (is) ≠ **e** (and). The accent is the whole difference: **Marco è alto e simpatico**.
- **ho, hai, ha, hanno** start with a silent **h**. Never pronounce it.

## Curiosità

That silent **h** is pure spelling engineering: it keeps **ho** (I have) apart from **o** (or), **hai** from **ai** (to the), **ha** from **a** (to) and **hanno** from **anno** (year). And in Rome and the South, **stare** is used a lot more like Spanish *estar* — you'll hear **sto a casa** where someone from Milan would say **sono a casa**.

## Il trucco

Spanish *estar* for a place or a state → **essere** (**sono a casa, sono stanco**). Keep **stare** for health (*¿cómo estás?* → **come stai?**) and for *estoy comiendo* → **sto mangiando**.
