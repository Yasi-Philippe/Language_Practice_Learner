# "La mia casa": the article Spanish lost

> Italian puts an article in front of the possessive: **la mia casa**, **il tuo libro**. Spanish used to do the same.

## La regola

| | m. sing. | f. sing. | m. plur. | f. plur. |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| my | il mio | la mia | i miei | le mie |
| your | il tuo | la tua | i tuoi | le tue |
| his, her, your (Lei) | il suo | la sua | i suoi | le sue |
| our | il nostro | la nostra | i nostri | le nostre |
| your (voi) | il vostro | la vostra | i vostri | le vostre |
| their | il loro | la loro | i loro | le loro |

## Come in spagnolo

- **suo** is as ambiguous as *su*: his, her, its, your (formal). Italian clears it up the same way: **di lui, di lei** (*de él, de ella*).
- The possessive agrees with the thing owned, not the owner: **Marco e la sua casa**, **Giulia e il suo libro**.
- **Mamma mia!** = *¡Madre mía!* — possessive after the noun, no article, in both languages.

## Diverso dallo spagnolo

- The article is normal: *mi casa* → **la mia casa**, *tus amigos* → **i tuoi amici**.
- One family member, singular, no frills → no article: **mia madre, tuo fratello**. But **i miei fratelli** (plural), **la mia mamma** (affectionate), **il mio fratellino** (diminutive), **il loro padre** (with **loro**, always).
- **loro** never changes and always keeps its article.

## Occhio!

- **i miei** on its own means "my parents": **vivo ancora con i miei**.
- After the noun there's no article: **a casa mia** = *en mi casa*, literally *en casa mía*.

## Curiosità

Old Spanish said it the Italian way. In the *Cantar de mio Cid* (around 1200), the Cid says goodbye to "**la mi mugier** tan complida" — "my so perfect wife", article included. Asturian still says *la mio casa*. Spanish dropped the article; Italian kept it.

## Il trucco

Translate Spanish *mi* as "**the my**": **la mia, il mio**. Then drop the article only for one plain family member.
