# The past you already know

> **ho mangiato** is *he comido*, piece by piece. The surprise is *when* Italians use it.

## La regola

**avere** (present) + past participle:

| verb ends in | participle | example |
|---|---|---|
| -are | **-ato** | parlare → **ho parlato** |
| -ere | **-uto** | ricevere → **ho ricevuto** |
| -ire | **-ito** | dormire → **ho dormito** |

## Come in spagnolo

- Same building blocks: *he hablado* = **ho parlato**, *has dormido* = **hai dormito**.
- The participles rhyme: *-ado* → **-ato**, *-ido* → **-ito**.
- With **avere** the participle doesn't change: **Maria ha mangiato** = *María ha comido*.

## Diverso dallo spagnolo

- Italians use it for yesterday too: *ayer comí pizza* → **ieri ho mangiato la pizza**. In everyday Italian it covers both *he comido* and *comí*.
- *-er* verbs take **-uto**, not *-ido*: **venduto, ricevuto, creduto, potuto, voluto**.

## Occhio!

- Not every verb uses **avere**: verbs of movement and change take **essere** (two categories from now).
- Many common verbs have irregular participles — **fatto, detto, visto**… That's the very next category.

## Curiosità

Italian does have its own *comí*: the **passato remoto** (**mangiai, parlai**). In the North it has practically vanished from speech; in Sicily and much of the South people still say **ieri mangiai** where someone from Milan says **ieri ho mangiato**. Everyone meets it in novels and fairy tales.

## Il trucco

Any finished event in Spanish — *comí* or *he comido* — becomes **ho mangiato**. (Spanish *comía* is another story: that's the imperfetto.)
