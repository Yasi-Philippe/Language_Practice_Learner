# Verbs with hidden pronouns

> **Ce l'ho fatta!** — "I made it!". Some Italian verbs carry pronouns that don't refer to anything in particular; they're simply part of the verb. Spanish has a few of these too.

## La regola

| verb | means | example |
|---|---|---|
| **farcela** | to manage | **ce la faccio**, **ce l'ho fatta** |
| **andarsene** | to leave | **me ne vado**, **se n'è andato** |
| **cavarsela** | to get by | **me la cavo** |
| **prendersela** | to take offence | **se la prende** |
| **metterci** | to take (time) | **ci metto un'ora** |
| **avercela con** | to be cross with | **ce l'hai con me?** |
| **smetterla** | to stop it | **smettila!** |
| **sentirsela** | to feel up to | **non me la sento** |

## Come in spagnolo

- Spanish has its own built-in *la / las*: *arreglárselas* = **cavarsela**, *tomarla con alguien* = **avercela con qualcuno**.
- **andarsene** = *irse*: **me ne vado** = *me voy*.

## Diverso dallo spagnolo

- Italian stacks **ci, ne, la** in a fixed order: **ce la faccio, me ne vado, me la cavo**.
- In the past, the **la** makes the participle feminine: **ce l'ho fatta**.

## Occhio!

- **ce l'ho** (from **avercela**) also simply means "I've got it": **hai la chiave? sì, ce l'ho**.
- The negative imperative has two orders: **non prendertela** = **non te la prendere**.

## Curiosità

The empty "la" puzzles every learner: what exactly is "it" in **ce la faccio**? Nothing in particular — and Spanish does exactly the same in *arreglárselas* or *tomarla con alguien*. Both languages love a pronoun that points at the whole situation.

## Il trucco

Learn them as whole words, pronoun chain included: **ce-la-faccio**, **me-ne-vado**, **me-la-cavo**, **ce-l'ho-con-te**.
